Eye To Ad Media Publishes Review Response Guidance for Owner-Operated Businesses

The guidance addresses the negative review specifically, and the responses that make a bad situation measurably worse.

Eye To Ad Media has published review response guidance for owner-operated businesses, focusing specifically on negative reviews and the response patterns that compound the damage.

The agency's central point is that owners misidentify the audience. A response to a negative review is read overwhelmingly by prospective customers, not by the person who wrote it.

"You're not writing to the reviewer. That relationship is usually over," said Zachary Tye Wennstedt, founder and chief executive of Eye To Ad Media. "You're writing to the person reading it three months from now, deciding whether to call you."

What makes a response worse than no response

  • Length. A four-paragraph rebuttal signals that the business is aggrieved. Nobody finishes it, and the length alone is read as defensiveness.
  • Relitigating the facts. The reader cannot adjudicate and will not try. They are assessing tone, not evidence.
  • Questioning whether they were a real customer. Occasionally true, almost always a bad look.
  • Copy-paste responses. Identical wording across several reviews is visible in a single scroll and reads as automation.
  • Disclosing private details. In regulated sectors this can be a compliance problem as well as a reputational one.

What works

The agency's recommended shape is short: acknowledge the experience described, correct any clear factual error in one calm sentence, offer a direct route to resolve it, and stop.

"Three sentences. A name and a number. Done," Wennstedt said. "The reader is looking for evidence that a real person is paying attention. That's the whole test."

On volume and recency

The guidance also notes that a steady flow of recent reviews matters more than a large total accumulated years ago, and that the most reliable way to get them is asking every satisfied customer at the moment the work is finished — rather than incentivizing reviews, which breaches most platforms' terms.

Frequently asked

Should a business respond to negative reviews?

Yes, in nearly all cases. A response is read by prospective customers far more often than by the reviewer, and an unanswered complaint reads as either agreement or indifference. The response is written for the next reader, not for the person who left it.

How should a business respond to an unfair review?

Briefly, factually and without arguing. Acknowledge that the experience described was not what the business intends, correct any factual error in one sentence without heat, and offer a direct route to resolve it offline. Long rebuttals persuade nobody and make the business look worse than the review did.

Can a business remove a negative review?

Only where it violates the platform's policies — fake reviews, reviews of the wrong business, content that breaches guidelines. Platforms do not remove reviews for being unflattering or inaccurate, and services promising removal of legitimate reviews are generally either ineffective or in breach of platform terms.
