Showers4Less Outlines Specification Differences Between Barrier-Free Shower Kits as Demand for Accessible Bathing Grows

The supplier is publishing comparison guidance covering pan construction, threshold height, wall system compatibility and the subfloor work that determines whether a zero-entry installation is feasible.

Showers4Less Issuing organization September 24, 2026

DENVER, Colorado — September 24, 2026

Showers4Less, a supplier of barrier-free and accessible shower systems, has published specification comparison guidance for households and contractors selecting between shower kit configurations. The guidance addresses the technical differences that determine whether a given kit can be installed in a given bathroom — a question frequently discovered late, after a product has already been purchased.

The company reports that the most common cause of a stalled accessible bathroom project is not the fixture itself but the floor underneath it. A true zero-threshold entry requires the shower pan to sit at or below the surrounding floor level, which in most existing construction means work to the subfloor.

Where specifications actually differ

Threshold height

The distinction between a barrier-free, low-threshold and curbed shower is measured in inches and determines who can use it. A wheelchair or rolling shower chair requires an entry at or very near floor level. A user who is ambulatory but unsteady may be adequately served by a low threshold, which is considerably simpler to install. Establishing which of these is actually required — now and over the expected life of the installation — is the first decision, and it constrains everything after it.

Pan construction

Shower pans differ in material, in how they handle drainage slope, and in how they are supported. A pan engineered to carry the weight of a seated user and an assisting caregiver has different structural requirements from a standard residential pan. Drainage configuration also varies: some designs slope to a center drain, others to a linear drain at one edge, and the choice affects both the subfloor work and the usable floor area.

Wall system compatibility

Wall systems and pans are engineered as matched sets. Mixing components across manufacturers frequently produces sealing problems at the joint between pan and wall, which is the point most likely to fail and the most expensive place to discover a failure.

Reinforcement for grab bars

Grab bars are only as sound as what they are anchored into. Wall systems designed for accessibility include blocking or reinforcement at the positions where bars are typically mounted. Retrofitting reinforcement into a finished wall is possible but means opening it again.

Specification checklist before purchase

  • Required entry height: zero threshold, low threshold, or curbed
  • Access method: ambulatory, walker, wheelchair, or rolling shower chair
  • Subfloor type and whether it can be recessed
  • Existing drain location and whether it can be moved
  • Pan load rating for seated use and caregiver assistance
  • Wall system matched to the pan by the same manufacturer
  • Reinforcement present at intended grab bar positions
  • Slip resistance rating of the pan surface when wet

The subfloor question

The company advises that the feasibility question should be settled before any product is selected.

In construction with a wood-framed floor, recessing a pan to achieve a level entry generally means modifying the joists or the subfloor, which requires assessment of whether the structure can accommodate it. In slab-on-grade construction, achieving a true zero threshold may require cutting the slab, which is a substantially larger undertaking. In either case, a low-threshold configuration may be the practical answer where a zero threshold is not.

"People buy the kit first and find out about the floor second," said Zachary Tye Wennstedt, who operates Showers4Less. "It should be the other way round. Find out what your floor will let you do, then buy the thing that fits it."
